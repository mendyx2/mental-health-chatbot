# Symptoms of mental health problems and the conditions they are commonly linked to

Mental health problems show up as changes in feelings, thinking, behaviour and the body. No single symptom proves a condition. What matters is which features appear together, how long they last (usually two weeks or more) and whether they interfere with school, work, family life or self-care. Only a qualified health professional can confirm a diagnosis. The information below is a preliminary screening guide.

## Low mood and feeling depressed
Features: persistent sadness or emptiness, loss of interest in things once enjoyed, feeling worthless or guilty, crying easily, tiredness, changes in sleep or appetite, poor concentration, and thoughts that life is not worth living. Commonly linked to: depression. Several of these features lasting two weeks or more suggest the person should see a doctor or counsellor.

## Being moody and mood swings
Features: quick changes between irritability, sadness, anger and calm, snapping at people, and difficulty controlling reactions. Mood swings can come from stress, poor sleep, hormones, substance use, or a mood disorder. Commonly linked to: stress and anger problems, depression, or bipolar disorder when swings are extreme, last for days and include periods of very high energy.

## Laughing excessively and unusually high mood
Features: laughing or giggling for no clear reason or far more than the situation calls for, feeling unusually happy, excited or powerful, talking very fast, needing little sleep without feeling tired, restlessness and risky spending or behaviour. Commonly linked to: a manic or hypomanic episode (bipolar disorder), the effect of drugs or alcohol, or severe stress. When it lasts several days and the person sleeps very little, a doctor should see them soon.

## Abnormal behaviour
Features: behaving in ways that are strange or out of character, talking to oneself, being unusually suspicious, aggression, wandering, neglecting washing and eating, or speech that does not make sense. Commonly linked to: psychosis (for example schizophrenia), severe depression or mania, substance effects, or a medical illness such as infection or head injury. Sudden abnormal behaviour needs prompt medical assessment.

## Seeing or hearing things that others do not
Features: hearing voices, seeing things that are not there, or strong beliefs that others are watching or plotting against the person. Commonly linked to: psychosis. This needs a doctor's assessment soon and is treated as high risk in this chatbot.

## Anxiety and panic
Features: constant worry, feeling on edge, racing heart, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, fear of something terrible happening, and avoiding places or situations. Commonly linked to: generalised anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety or stress.

## Sleep, appetite and energy changes
Sleeping far too little or too much, losing appetite or overeating, and constant tiredness often appear together with depression, anxiety or mania and are useful clues when combined with other features.

## Withdrawal and poor concentration
Avoiding friends and family, staying alone, stopping usual activities, forgetfulness and difficulty focusing are common early signs that something is wrong, especially when they are new for the person.

## Important note
A list of features is not a diagnosis. Many people have some of these experiences at times. The chatbot only gives a preliminary indication so that the person knows when to seek proper help.
